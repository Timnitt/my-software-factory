How the Greeks Built: Research Findings

A short reading list on the design and construction processes of ancient Greek architecture: how buildings were planned, proportioned, lifted and organised.

Reliability note: I could read abstracts, catalogue records and press coverage, but not the full text of most items below. Summaries reflect those sources. Check the papers themselves before citing them.

1. Design: drawings and proportion

Journal / magazine

The Construction Plans for the Temple of Apollo at Didyma

Lothar Haselberger, Scientific American 253(6), December 1985.

Haselberger found life-size architectural drawings incised in the marble walls of the temple's inner court (c. 300 B.C.). They show that Greek "blueprints" were drawn at full scale on the building itself, and one drawing of entasis (the slight swelling of a column) helps reconstruct a method Vitruvius attributes to Daphnis and Paeonius.

Scientific American page

Journal article, two parts

Doric Measure and Architectural Design 1 and 2

Mark Wilson Jones, American Journal of Archaeology 104.1 (2000) and 105.4 (2001).

Part 1 uses the relief from Salamis as evidence. Part 2 argues that classical Doric temples were designed with a mix of proportional and modular principles, with the triglyph width as the key module, consistent with Vitruvius. A Brazilian study tested the idea on ten peripteral Doric temples and found it pertinent, with qualifications.

Book

Ancient Greek Architects at Work: Problems of Structure and Design

J. J. Coulton, Elek 1977; Cornell University Press 1982.

Looks at Greek building from the architect's point of view, using both ancient texts and the surviving buildings. Includes chapters on "the problem of design" and "the problem of scale." I only saw catalogue entries, so I can't say what it concludes about modules.

2. Construction: lifting and setting stone

Journal article

Lifting in Early Greek Architecture

J. J. Coulton, Journal of Hellenic Studies 94 (1974), pp. 1–19.

Questions the handbook view that Archaic builders used the compound-pulley cranes described by Vitruvius and Hero, and asks whether the earth ramp used for the architraves at Ephesos was an exception. It treats lifting as a question in the history of technology.

Cambridge abstract

Journal article

Interpreting rope channels: lifting, setting and the birth of Greek monumental architecture

Alessandro Pierattini, Annual of the British School at Athens (2019), DOI 10.1017/S0068245419000030.

Argues that twin grooves on ashlar blocks from Isthmia and Corinth (c. 700–650 B.C.) were for rope-lifting with a frame, not a winch crane, and also helped set blocks tightly against neighbours. That would place lifting devices about 150 years earlier than Coulton's view. Details here come from press coverage, not the article.

Cambridge blog summary · Smithsonian coverage

Book

The Origins of Greek Temple Architecture

Alessandro Pierattini, Cambridge University Press, 2022.

Traces pre-Archaic temples from the 11th to the mid-7th century B.C. A reviewer reports that full ashlar walls and terracotta roofing appear at Corinth and Isthmia in the 7th century, pointing to technical expertise developing earlier.

Cambridge page

Book / popular

Parthenon stone-working (Manolis Korres)

Korres, The Stones of the Parthenon; PBS NOVA, "Secrets of the Parthenon."

Korres's reconstruction has marble quarried at Pentelicon, roughed out at the quarry, carted to Athens and hauled up the Acropolis. He argues that fluting the columns was perhaps as laborious as the other stages combined. Lifting methods are inferred, not documented. My search found no peer-reviewed Korres article, only books and popular coverage.

3. Refinements and organisation

Journal / thesis

Entasis: perception or engineering?

Thompson et al., Spatial Vision 20(6), 2007 · Pakkanen, Archeologia e Calcolatori (1996) · Lorente, Univ. of Ottawa thesis (1979).

The Spatial Vision study treats entasis as an optical correction that the evidence also supports as an engineering feature, and dates its likely first use to the later Temple of Aphaia at Aegina, c. 490s B.C. Pakkanen examines the columns of the Temple of Athena Alea at Tegea. I saw only snippets and catalogue records.

Spatial Vision paper (PDF) · Pakkanen · Lorente thesis

Book

The Greek Temple Builders at Epidauros

Alison Burford, Liverpool University Press, 1969.

A social and economic study of 4th-century B.C. building at the Asklepios sanctuary, based on the surviving building accounts. A later German work by Prignitz (2014) re-edits the inscriptions and notes that the temple took four years and nine months to build.

BMCR review of Prignitz

Takeaways

  • Design was physical: full-size drawings on the building itself, plus proportional and module-based rules.
  • Lifting technology is debated: Coulton's late-crane view versus Pierattini's earlier rope-and-frame systems.
  • Refinements such as entasis may have served both appearance and structure.
  • Epidauros gives rare documentary evidence of how a project was managed and paid for.
